How long does America care about an earthquake?

When the ground shakes, people rush to Wikipedia. We counted how many came, and how quickly they left.

Scroll to follow the story ↓

On July 4, 2019, a magnitude 6.4 earthquake shook Ridgecrest, California. A day and a half later a far larger one followed, at magnitude 7.1. On the busiest day, 31,407 people opened the Wikipedia article about it.

Then they left. One week after the peak, the article drew 3,147 readers. Nine in ten had moved on.

Is that normal? Here are three more: Anchorage in 2018, Salt Lake City in 2020, and the New Jersey quake that rattled New York in 2024. Different sizes, different crowds.

Now line them up by their busiest day and measure each one against its own peak. Three of the four lines nearly collapse into one. Salt Lake City is the odd one out, with bursts of readers coming back. Even so, a week out, between 71 and 96 percent of readers are gone.

What the crowd does not follow is the size of the quake. Ridgecrest and Anchorage were both magnitude 7.1, yet Ridgecrest drew almost three times the readers. New Jersey's 4.8 released roughly 2,800 times less energy than Anchorage and still drew more readers. It shook the most crowded corridor in the country.

So here is the pattern. How many people look depends on how many people felt it. How long they keep looking barely changes. By the very next day, 59 to 66 percent of readers were already gone.

How this was made

  • Two sources, one glue. Earthquake times and sizes come from the USGS earthquake feeds. Reader counts come from the Wikimedia Pageviews API. The glue that joins them is the date.
  • The story is fixed, the map is live. The four reader curves were fetched once and saved inside this page, so the story always works. Only the map calls out to the internet.
  • Reader counts are daily views of each quake's English Wikipedia article by people (not bots), for 31 days starting on the day of the first quake. Days follow UTC time, which is why a quake that struck in the evening shows its peak on the "next" day.
  • One swap. The plan named the 2020 Puerto Rico earthquakes. Its Wikipedia article was renamed partway through that month, which splits the reader counts across two titles, so Salt Lake City 2020 stands in. Renamed pages are a common trap with this data.
  • Four earthquakes is an illustration, not a study. A real piece would test this on dozens.
  • Map shapes are from Natural Earth. No keys, accounts or paid services were used.
